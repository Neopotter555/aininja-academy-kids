# AI Engineer for Kids

## Project-first course for ages 10-16

This course teaches children to use AI safely, think clearly, test ideas, and build one complete game. The language stays simple enough for a five-year-old to follow, while the projects can be extended for older learners.

## Course promise

By the end, each learner or small team will have built and presented a playable **AI Safety Quest** game. The game teaches another child how to ask AI for help, protect private information, check answers, respond to unsafe output, and improve a weak result.

The larger goal is not to make children memorize technical words. It is to help them form the habits of an AI engineer:

1. Understand the problem.
2. Break it into small pieces.
3. Give clear instructions.
4. Test what comes back.
5. Look for mistakes and missing viewpoints.
6. Improve one thing at a time.
7. Keep people safe and in control.
8. Explain what was built and why.

## Audience and delivery

- Ages: 10-16
- Format: 6 sessions, 60 minutes each
- Grouping: pairs or teams of three
- Practice-to-theory ratio: about 80% building and 20% explanation
- Required experience: none
- Recommended tools: paper, markers, presentation software, Scratch or another teacher-approved block-coding tool, and one adult-operated AI account for demonstrations
- Final project: a playable branching game with at least five decisions, three safety lessons, a scoring system, and a reflection screen

### Special Codex access rule

- **Ages 10-12:** Learners do not create personal Codex accounts, share an adult's login, or directly operate Codex. They plan prompts, predict results, test the game, and suggest improvements while the teacher signs in, types or runs the task, approves every action, and reviews the result.
- **Ages 13-16:** Direct use requires parent or guardian permission, teacher or school approval, and active teacher supervision.
- **Every learner:** Never share account credentials or enter names, faces, voices, precise locations, secrets, health information, family information, or private schoolwork.
- **Thailand workshops:** Collect written guardian permission and provide a clear privacy notice. Re-check the service's current age rules and local requirements before every cohort.

### Two difficulty tracks

Every class shares the same story and project. The depth changes by age.

| Learners | Core track | Stretch track |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ages 10-12 | Use pictures, prompt cards, block coding, and short explanations. Work mostly in pairs. | Add one extra game level or a second ending. |
| Ages 13-16 | Add variables, reusable blocks, test tables, source checks, and written design choices. | Add adaptive difficulty, a simple rubric, or teacher-approved AI-generated hints. |

Do not divide learners by ability in public. Let everyone begin with the core track and choose a stretch challenge.

## The two memory stories

### Story 1: The Robot Puppy

“Imagine a very fast robot puppy. It has read many instruction cards. It wants to help, but it does not know what you mean unless you explain the job. Sometimes it confidently brings a slipper when you asked for a sandwich. A good AI engineer gives the puppy a clear job, shows an example, checks what it brings back, and tries again.”

This story teaches:

- AI predicts useful-looking answers; it does not think or feel like a person.
- Clear instructions and examples improve results.
- A confident answer can still be wrong.
- A human must check and decide.

### Story 2: The Backpack Rule

“Your name, address, school, passwords, private photos, and family details belong in a zipped backpack. We do not empty that backpack into a robot’s training box.”

Use the **SAFE** check before sending anything to AI:

- **S — Secrets stay secret.** Never share passwords, addresses, phone numbers, school details, private photos, or another person’s information.
- **A — Ask a trusted adult.** Stop when a request feels personal, frightening, unfair, or confusing.
- **F — Find the proof.** Check important claims with reliable sources or a knowledgeable adult.
- **E — Everyone deserves kindness.** Do not use AI to bully, cheat, impersonate, or harm.

Put the SAFE card beside every device during the course.

## The child-friendly AI engineer loop

Call this the **Build-a-Bridge Loop**:

1. **Pick the island — What are we trying to reach?** Say the goal in one sentence.
2. **Count the stepping stones — What small jobs are needed?** Break the goal into steps.
3. **Give the robot a map — What instruction and example will help?** Write a clear prompt.
4. **Walk across slowly — Does each part work?** Test one step at a time.
5. **Shake the bridge — Where could it fail?** Try unusual, unclear, and unsafe inputs.
6. **Fix one plank — What is the smallest useful change?** Improve and test again.
7. **Add a guardrail — How do we keep people safe?** Protect privacy and keep a human decision point.
8. **Show the map — Can someone else understand it?** Explain the build in simple words.

Avoid beginning with words such as “model,” “algorithm,” or “inference.” Teach the idea first through the story, then offer the correct term as an optional label.

## Standard 60-minute session rhythm

Use the same rhythm every time so learners know what comes next.

| Time | Activity | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0-4 min | Story and movement game | Introduce one memorable idea without a lecture. |
| 4-10 min | Teacher demonstration | Show the idea, including one deliberate mistake. |
| 10-44 min | Build challenge | Learners make something in pairs or teams. |
| 44-52 min | Swap and test | Another team tries to break or improve it. |
| 52-58 min | Fix one thing | Learners use feedback and test again. |
| 58-60 min | Exit ticket | Say: “I built…, I checked…, Next I would…” |

This keeps 48 of 60 minutes practical. No uninterrupted explanation should run longer than six minutes.

## Six-session curriculum

### Session 1 — Meet the Robot Puppy

**Big idea:** AI predicts answers from patterns. The child is the captain who chooses the mission and checks the result.

**Story:** The robot puppy fetches a red ball correctly three times, then brings a red apple too. In the AI Coding Cockpit, the child is the captain and the puppy is a fast helper.

**Build:** Explorer learners make a paper sorting robot. Builder learners make a Cosmic Hello App with one input, one button, and one visible result.

**Steps:**

1. Say the mission in one sentence.
2. Show the helper two or three examples.
3. Make the smallest version work.
4. Test it with one new or unusual example.
5. Fix one thing.
6. Repeat the same test and explain the evidence.

**Mini-project:** Pattern Detective or Cosmic Hello App.

**Thinking habit:** A pattern is a clue, not proof.

**Adaptive topics:** The AI Coding Cockpit, The Loop, and Cosmic Hello.

### Session 2 — Map It, Then Check It

**Big idea:** A useful prompt gives the robot a clear map. A confident answer still needs a truth check.

**Story:** A treasure hunter needs more than “take me there.” A loud parrot can also point toward the wrong treasure, so the map and the answer both need checking.

**Build:** Teams repair a vague prompt with GOAL, then inspect its answer with the Three-Lamp Check.

Use the **GOAL** prompt recipe:

- **G — Goal:** What should the AI help make or solve?
- **O — Objects and facts:** What information does it need?
- **A — Agreements:** What rules, limits, and safety boundaries apply?
- **L — Look:** What should the answer look like?

**Steps:**

1. Start with “Tell me about space.”
2. Add a specific goal.
3. Add the learner’s age and what they already know.
4. Add one safety or accuracy rule.
5. Ask for a format, such as three bullets or a tiny story.
6. Mark the answer red, yellow, or green.
7. Check one important claim with a reliable source or teacher.

Use the **Three-Lamp Check**:

1. **Red:** The answer could affect health, money, safety, or reputation. Stop and ask an adult or expert.
2. **Yellow:** It is a surprising, new, or unsourced fact. Check it.
3. **Green:** It is a low-stakes creative suggestion. Explore while staying kind.

**Mini-project:** GOAL Prompt Map plus one Fact-Check Card.

**Thinking habit:** Clear instructions help; evidence decides. “I don’t know yet” is an intelligent answer.

**Adaptive topics:** Prompt Engineering That Works and A Second Pair of Robot Eyes.

### Session 3 — Protect People and Build Kindly

**Big idea:** Safe engineers protect private information and choose ideas that are fair, useful, and kind.

**Story:** Private details stay inside a zipped backpack. A magic crayon can draw ten playgrounds, but a thoughtful child checks whether everyone can use them.

**Build:** Repair an unsafe prompt with fictional details, then create and fairness-check a Kind Story Machine.

**Steps:**

1. Highlight personal or unnecessary information.
2. Remove it or replace it with invented details.
3. Run the SAFE check.
4. Build a story from hero, goal, obstacle, helper, and ending cards.
5. Ask who is included, who is missing, and whether any choice could hurt someone.
6. Improve one part and explain the human decision.

**Mini-project:** Privacy Shield plus Kind Story Machine.

**Thinking habit:** Share only what the job needs, and ask who might be missing.

**Adaptive topics:** Privacy, fairness, and an optional teacher-led risk review.

### Session 4 — Build in Tiny Loops

**Big idea:** Engineers build, test, debug, and improve one small working piece at a time.

**Story:** Cross a river one stepping stone at a time. If one stone wobbles, make the bug show itself and follow its muddy footprints.

**Build:** Create the required three-question SAFE Quiz that will become the first playable path of the final AI Safety Quest.

**Steps:**

1. Write the player’s goal.
2. Draw the start, three questions, feedback, score, and ending.
3. Build only the start and first question.
4. Test the click path.
5. Add the remaining questions and score.
6. Reproduce one bug.
7. Fix the smallest cause.
8. Repeat the same test.

**Mini-project:** Three-question SAFE Quiz—the capstone foundation.

**Thinking habit:** Small working pieces beat one giant unfinished idea.

**Adaptive topics:** The Loop, Debugging Without Panic, Building Tiny Apps, and Practice Labs.

**Alternative:** Habit Rocket may replace the quiz only when the teacher provides a ready-made AI Safety Quest starter for Session 5.

### Session 5 — Build AI Safety Quest

**Big idea:** A useful product starts with one player, one mission, testable choices, and guardrails.

**Story:** Build one working lemonade stand before drawing a giant shopping mall. The player enters Robot City with a map, truth lamp, privacy backpack, and kindness shield.

**Build:** Expand the Session 4 quiz into a branching AI Safety Quest.

Required game features:

- A title and clear mission
- At least five player decisions
- At least three SAFE lessons
- One privacy challenge
- One “check the answer” challenge
- One kindness or fairness challenge
- A score, badge, or progress meter
- Helpful feedback after each choice
- At least two endings
- A final reflection: “What will you do before trusting AI?”

**Steps:**

1. Choose a mission: rescue a library, prepare a space trip, solve a mystery, or protect a magical forest.
2. Reuse the working quiz path from Session 4.
3. Add two decisions so the game has at least five.
4. Mark where privacy, truth, and kindness appear.
5. Add the second ending and final reflection.
6. Test every choice once.

**Core-track tool:** Scratch or presentation slides with linked buttons.

**Stretch-track option:** Use variables, reusable blocks, a question bank, or teacher-approved AI-generated hints. AI must never ask the player for personal information.

**Thinking habit:** Safety belongs inside the design, not in tiny words at the end.

**Adaptive topics:** Building Tiny SaaS and Web Apps, The Loop, and Practice Labs.

### Session 6 — Test, Ship, and Showcase

**Big idea:** Engineers use testers and a second pair of robot eyes, then show proof that the project works.

**Story:** A bridge builder tests with toy elephants, mice, wind, and a checker robot before people cross.

**Build:** Peer-test AI Safety Quest, compare one reviewer opinion, fix the most important issue, retest, and present.

**Team test sheet:**

| Test | Question |
| --- | --- |
| New-player test | Can someone understand the mission without help? |
| Wrong-choice test | Does an unsafe choice teach rather than shame? |
| Privacy test | Does the game avoid requesting real personal information? |
| Truth test | Does the game remind players to verify important claims? |
| Fairness test | Are characters and choices respectful and inclusive? |
| Edge-case test | What happens if a player repeats, skips, or chooses an unexpected path? |
| Learning test | Can the player name one SAFE rule afterward? |

**Steps:**

1. Observe another child playing silently.
2. Write down where they pause or become confused.
3. Ask a second reviewer for risks, but treat the opinion as a clue rather than proof.
4. Choose the most important problem.
5. Fix it and repeat the same test.
6. Run the shipping checklist: app opens, buttons work, text is readable, private data is absent, and limits are explained.
7. Present the game in three minutes.

**Showcase sentence frame:**

> We built ___ for ___. The hardest problem was ___. Our safety guardrail is ___. A tester found ___. We improved it by ___.

**Thinking habit:** A bug is a clue that helps the next version.

**Adaptive topics:** A Second Pair of Robot Eyes, Shipping a Real Project, and The Teacher Loop.

## Optional project bank

These are short builds for extra classes, early finishers, clubs, or camps.

### 1. Silly Prompt Repair Shop

Learners repair unclear prompts and explain which GOAL ingredient was missing.

### 2. Hallucination Detective Board

Learners collect fictional “confident parrot” answers, mark the suspicious clues, and describe how they would check each claim.

### 3. Emotion-Free Robot Translator

Learners rewrite rude or confusing messages in a calm, kind tone. Remind them that AI can copy emotional language but does not feel emotions.

### 4. Eco-Invention Studio

Teams ask for ideas to reduce classroom waste, compare options, select one using simple criteria, and build a paper prototype.

### 5. Homework Helper Rules

Learners design a helper that gives hints, asks questions, and explains steps without completing the learner’s work for them.

### 6. Bias Goggles Challenge

Teams inspect a fictional recommendation list and ask: Who is included? Who is missing? What information might change the answer?

### 7. AI Zoo Guide

Learners create a guide for imaginary animals, then separate creative details from real animal facts that require checking.

## Final project rubric

Score each area from 1 to 4. Use the rubric for feedback, not competition.

| Area | 1 — Starting | 2 — Growing | 3 — Working | 4 — Thoughtful |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Problem | Goal is unclear. | Goal needs explanation. | Player and goal are clear. | Goal is clear, useful, and focused. |
| Build | Few choices work. | One path works. | Required paths and feedback work. | Game is polished and handles unusual choices. |
| AI thinking | Steps are hard to follow. | Some steps are visible. | Team used the Build-a-Bridge Loop. | Team explains trade-offs and iterations clearly. |
| Safety | Safety appears only at the end. | One safety rule is shown. | Privacy, checking, and kindness are built in. | Guardrails teach clearly without fear or shame. |
| Testing | No outside test. | One test with little change. | Peer test produced an improvement. | Several tests led to specific improvements. |
| Explanation | Team names features. | Team partly explains choices. | Team explains user, problem, safety, and fix. | Every member can explain and answer questions. |

Completion target: mostly level 3. Level 4 is an optional stretch, not the price of success.

## Adaptive AI Engineering 101 syllabus

These nine topics are teaching ingredients folded into the six-session safety course; they are not nine extra classes. Use the same idea with two depths: the Explorer track for ages 10–12 uses pictures, metaphors, paired work, and guided steps; the Builder track for ages 13–16 adds success criteria, stronger testing, written evidence, and optional teacher-managed coding agents.

### Course structure

| Session | Core focus | Adaptive topics | Practical outcome |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Meet the Robot Puppy | AI Coding Cockpit and Loop introduction | Test a pattern helper or Cosmic Hello App and improve one result. |
| 2 | Map It, Then Check It | Prompt Engineering and Second Pair of Robot Eyes | Repair a GOAL prompt and verify its answer. |
| 3 | Protect People and Build Kindly | Privacy, fairness, and optional risk review | Repair unsafe data and build an inclusive idea. |
| 4 | Build in Tiny Loops | Loop, Debugging, Tiny Apps, and Practice Labs | Build the three-question SAFE Quiz as the capstone’s first playable path. |
| 5 | Build AI Safety Quest | Tiny Apps, Loop, and debugging | Expand the quiz into a five-choice, two-ending game. |
| 6 | Test, Ship, and Showcase | Second Pair of Robot Eyes and Shipping | Peer-test, fix, verify, and present the game. |

Delivery contract:

- **Six classes total:** run weekly, twice weekly, or as two classes per day in a three-day camp.
- **Sixty minutes per class:** four minutes of story, six minutes of demonstration, 48 minutes of building/testing/fixing, and a two-minute exit ticket.
- **Six hours total:** Session 4 creates the capstone foundation, Session 5 completes it, and Session 6 tests and presents it.
- **Teacher Loop:** use it as the facilitation rhythm in all six classes, not as a seventh learner session.

Every module ends with the same three pieces of proof:

1. A working click path or repeatable demonstration.
2. A visible result or test record.
3. A learner explanation of what changed, what was checked, and what should happen next.

Safety boundaries apply throughout: teachers manage coding-agent accounts; learners use fictional or sample data; no real payments, production databases, child accounts, secrets, faces, voices, precise locations, or private schoolwork are used in class; and public sharing requires teacher and guardian approval.

## Teacher safety and facilitation rules

1. Use a school- or teacher-approved AI service and follow its current age and consent requirements.
2. For ages 10-12, the teacher signs in and operates Codex. Learners may plan, predict, test, and improve, but they do not create personal accounts, share the teacher's login, or directly operate Codex.
3. For ages 13-16, direct Codex use requires parent or guardian permission and active teacher supervision.
4. Do not enter real names, contact details, precise locations, faces, voices, health details, private schoolwork, or family information.
5. Use fictional or teacher-provided data in every demonstration.
6. Do not use AI as a therapist, doctor, authority, disciplinarian, or private confidant.
7. A teacher reviews generated content before it is shown widely or published.
8. Do not publish a child’s game, image, name, or voice without the required guardian and school permission.
9. Teach learners to report disturbing, sexual, hateful, manipulative, or frightening output immediately. They should close or cover the screen and call an adult, not investigate it alone.
10. Do not grade learners on whether AI produced a “perfect” answer. Grade their questions, checking, improvements, safety decisions, and explanations.
11. Keep a non-AI path available so every learner can participate if consent, access, connectivity, or comfort is different.

## Assessment without heavy theory

Use evidence from the work rather than a long written test:

- Prompt cards show whether the learner can give clear instructions.
- Fact-check cards show whether the learner can pause and verify.
- Privacy repairs show whether the learner can remove unnecessary personal details.
- Build logs show whether the learner can test and improve.
- The final game shows whether the learner can combine problem-solving with safety.
- The showcase explanation reveals whether the learner understands the choices they made.

Each learner keeps a one-page engineer log:

```text
Today our goal was:
We broke it into:
We asked or instructed:
The first result did:
We found this problem:
We changed:
Our safety check was:
Next time we would:
```

## What success looks like

At the end of the course, a learner should be able to say:

- “AI is a fast pattern helper, not an all-knowing person.”
- “I can give it a clear job and example.”
- “I check important answers instead of trusting confidence.”
- “I keep private information in my backpack.”
- “I ask an adult when something feels unsafe or confusing.”
- “I can break a big idea into small parts, test it, and improve it.”
- “I made a game that teaches someone else to use AI safely.”
